Papers and events·Nov 1

Jason Hickey proposes agent-authored software where humans review only formal specifications

Continuous integration checks replace code review: on a zero-copy authenticated message router, the kernel check passed in 42 ms and mutation testing killed all 10 synthetic bugs. The paper builds on Lean 4 artifacts (salt, x86lean, vir) and contrasts Lean subtypes with Computational Type Theory subset types; several components remain proposals.

Papers and events·Nov 3

Paper determines order of magnitude of low moments of random multiplicative sums

Christopher Atherfold, Maxim Gerspach and Jad Hamdan study the 2q-th moments, for q from 0 to 1, of sums up to x of a random, completely multiplicative function with independent, uniform ±1 values at the primes. A transition from better-than-square-root cancellation to growth like a power of log x occurs when q crosses 1/2.

⊢Checked against: arXiv:2611.05492

Papers and events·Nov 2

Researchers bring effect handlers to JAX, backed by Lean 4 proofs

Oghenevwogaga Ebresafe, Daniel Zheng, Gordon Plotkin, and Ningning Xie compile effect handlers to restricted, first-order accelerator languages via a translation that statically eliminates the handlers and preserves typing and semantics, with proofs fully mechanized in Lean 4. The design is implemented as a library for JAX, a Python domain-specific language for accelerator programming.

⊢Checked against: arXiv:2611.03918

Papers and events·Oct 30

Darius Foo and coauthors reconstruct the Iris base logic in Lean

Indirection Theory is simpler than Iris, thanks to ordinary dependent types, but less expressive; the authors' lightweight Lean mechanisation generalises it to Iris-style resources. Two case studies, including a program logic complete enough to verify concurrent and higher-order programs, show the base logic is expressive and faithful to Iris.

⊢Checked against: arXiv:2610.41202

Papers and events·Oct 29

GenProof paper on verifying Minecraft redstone circuits uses Lean's bv_decide

Mike Murunov and Maksim Borisov's gate accepts a redstone layout only with a machine-checkable certificate of equivalence to its Verilog specification. Lean 4 re-proves the claim, unfolding it to one 4-bit equation per node and calling bv_decide, which bit-blasts, runs the CaDiCaL solver and checks its proof with a verified checker.

Papers and events·Oct 22

Alexander Meiburg disproves perfect randomized composition for total functions, checked in Lean

Perfect randomized composition (a composition's randomized query complexity is always at least a constant times the product of its factors' complexities) was known to fail for partial Boolean functions but open for total ones. The main result is machine-checked in Lean 4 as a little-o statement on a subsequence of the construction.

⊢Checked against: arXiv:2610.31638·paperswithlean.com

Papers and events·Oct 20

Carlo Ciliberto, Alessandro Rudi, Enrico Tassi introduce Proof-Carrying Contracts for AI agents

An execution system called the Airlock runs a tool call only if the Lean kernel accepts the agent's proof that the call preserves the agreed constraints in every case its model allows. A Lean 4 mechanization proves the constraints hold at every instant of execution, even for an adversarial agent, under stated assumptions.

⊢Checked against: arXiv:2610.28484·consequential-ai/proof-carrying-contracts·paperswithlean.com

Papers and events·Oct 22

Leo de Moura publishes MiniForth, a Lean tutorial for programming language semantics

MiniForth's Forth-inspired teaching language, whose programs act on a stack, has arithmetic, DUP, SWAP, DROP, calls, and conditionals. The tutorial embeds it as Lean functions (a shallow embedding) and as data (a deep embedding), proves program equivalences, and adds a second pass where grind handles routine reasoning; the build checks the examples and proofs.

Papers and events·Aug 29

Maher Kallel and Mohamed El Louadi measure the audit burden of AI proofs

The paper studies ten results an OpenAI lab published in August 2026 with Lean 4 certificates: kernel-checked proofs total 20.6 MB against 55.6 KB of statements needing human audit (379 to 1), yet those statements contain 218 bespoke definitions. Four weeks later, one result remained disputed over whether its formalization meant what it claimed.
